Vietnam

Overview

A country shaped by history, resilience & rapid growth

Vietnam is famously known as a land of warm smiles, millions of buzzing scooters, and a vibrant street culture. Everyday life thrives outdoors, where locals gather on tiny plastic stools on the sidewalks to share fresh spring rolls, steaming bowls of phở, and crispy Vietnamese pancakes (bánh xèo).

Politically, Vietnam is a single-party socialist state led by the Communist Party of Vietnam. It runs on a hybrid economic model: the government keeps strong control over vital sectors like banking, energy, and telecom, while letting private companies and foreign factories grow rapidly.

Daily Life

Culture, Behavior & Sidewalk Rituals

Vietnamese daily life is strongly shaped by collectivism, practicality, and climate-adapted living. Family and local networks strongly influence social and economic decisions.

Coffee Culture

Social life is highly public—food stalls, plastic stools, and street cafés are central to daily interaction. This is powered by Cà phê sữa đá (Vietnamese iced milk coffee)—a strong brew made from slow-dripped, dark-roast Robusta beans mixed with sweet condensed milk over ice. Because Robusta beans contain nearly double the caffeine of standard Arabica beans, it packs an exceptionally powerful punch. Popular variations also include egg coffee and salted coffee.

Smoking Habits

Very often, a cup of street coffee comes paired with a cigarette. Vietnam remains one of the countries with the highest rates of male smokers in the world, sitting within the global top 15 nations. Recent healthcare data indicates that roughly 41% of Vietnamese adult men smoke, contrasting sharply with less than 1% of women in the country.

Staple Foods

Food is typically served family-style, with dishes placed in the center. Common staples include pho, spring rolls, bun cha, banh xeo, and rice-based dishes.

Scooters & Footwear

Scooters are the dominant transport mode due to affordability, flexibility in dense cities, and infrastructure designed around narrow streets. Sandals or slippers are widely used due to the hot and humid climate and frequent transitions between indoor and outdoor spaces.

Economy

From Central Planning to Export Powerhouse

Vietnam is widely considered one of the fastest-growing economies in Asia over the past two decades, with sustained GDP growth typically around 5–7% annually (averaging 6% to 7% every year). This easily beats mature regional neighbors like Thailand and Malaysia, which grow around 2.5% to 4.5% in industrial expansion and export growth over the same period.

Since the Đổi Mới reforms (1986), Vietnam has transitioned from a centrally planned economy to a “socialist-oriented market economy”, where private enterprise and foreign investment operate alongside strong state-owned enterprises in strategic sectors. A large part of this transformation is driven by integration into global manufacturing networks, becoming a key alternative to China in global supply chains for companies diversifying production (the “China +1” strategy).

Global Export Rankings

Today, Vietnam is one of Asia's top export hubs. It is the world's 2nd largest exporter of smartphones—trailing only China, and ranking just ahead of India. This is heavily driven by tech giants like Samsung, which builds roughly half of its global phone supply inside Vietnamese factories. Furthermore, Vietnam is the 3rd largest exporter of clothing and textiles globally.

Labor & Minimum Wage

To ensure basic living standards within this production ecosystem, Vietnam splits its legal minimum wage into four geographic areas. The official minimum salary ranges from about €130 per month in rural villages up to €185 per month in major cities like Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi.

Key Growth Drivers:

  • Relocation of global supply chains (“China +1” strategy)
  • Electronics manufacturing hubs: Samsung, Apple suppliers, LG, Intel
  • Textiles & footwear industries: Nike, Adidas, and global apparel production networks
  • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): Highly targeted, export-oriented industrial zones
  • Urbanization & rising consumption: An expanding, dynamic middle class in major cities
History

Layered Influences & Resilience

The deep foundation of modern Vietnam is a long history of intense resilience, surviving shifting global powers while fiercely preserving its national identity.

111 BC – 939 AD (1000+ Years)
Imperial Chinese Rule

Long historical periods of Chinese rule shaped administration, Confucian values, and governance systems. Many cultural and bureaucratic structures still show influence today, though Vietnam preserved its cultural identity through constant resistance.

1858 – 1954
French Colonial Era

The colonial period subjected the people to harsh forced labor. It also introduced Western-style architecture, Catholic influence, coffee culture, and the Latin-based Vietnamese writing system (Quốc Ngữ), which replaced classical Chinese scripts.

1955 – 1975
USA / Vietnam War & Independence

The biggest test came in the mid-1900s when the United States intervened with massive military power to stop communism. This Cold War conflict between the communist North and US-supported South caused major destruction. Led by Hồ Chí Minh, Vietnamese forces fought a decades-long war, ultimately winning full independence (originally declared in 1945) and reunifying the country under communist leadership in 1975, deeply shaping modern national identity.

1975 – 1985
Post-Reunification Era

Reunification under a socialist state with centralized, Soviet-style planned governance over a war-torn, struggling economy.

1986 – Present
The Đổi Mới Reforms

The government launched the Đổi Mới (Renovation) reforms to save the economy. This policy completely shifted Vietnam away from central planning by allowing private businesses to open, removing government price controls, and opening borders to international trade and foreign investment, sparking the modern economic boom.

Religion

Beliefs, Ancestor Worship & Spiritual Diversity

Religion in Vietnam is unique because many people do not identify strongly with a single organized religion. Instead, spiritual life often combines ancestor worship, folk traditions, Buddhism, Confucian values, and Taoist beliefs.

Ancestor Worship

The most widespread spiritual practice in Vietnam is ancestor worship. Many families maintain home altars where offerings of fruit, flowers, incense, and food are presented to deceased relatives. Ancestors are believed to continue guiding and protecting the family.

The "Three Teachings"

Vietnamese culture has long blended Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism. Rather than competing religions, these traditions often coexist, influencing ethics, family values, education, and spiritual practices.

Buddhism

Buddhism is the largest organized religion in Vietnam and can be seen through thousands of temples and pagodas across the country. It plays an important role in festivals, daily rituals, and concepts of compassion and karma.

Christianity & Other Faiths

Catholicism was introduced during the French colonial period and remains the country's second-largest religion, with roughly 6–7% of the population identifying as Catholic. Vietnam is also home to unique indigenous religions such as Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo, alongside smaller Muslim, Protestant, and Hindu communities.

Interesting Facts

  • Many Vietnamese people visit both Buddhist temples and ancestor altars without seeing any contradiction.
  • The giant incense coils often seen hanging from temple ceilings are prayers intended to burn continuously for weeks.
  • Ancestor worship is so common that even many officially non-religious households maintain family altars.
  • Cao Đài, founded in southern Vietnam in 1926, combines elements of Buddhism, Christianity, Taoism, and Confucianism into a single faith.
Language

Communication

  • Official Language: Vietnamese, a tonal language utilizing 6 distinct tones.
  • Writing System: The Latin-based script Quốc Ngữ.
  • Linguistic Diversity: Home to over 100+ ethnic groups, each preserving their own distinct minority languages alongside the official tongue.
  • English Fluency: Usage is rapidly increasing among urban youth populations.
