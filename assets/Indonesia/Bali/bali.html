Bali

Bali is Indonesia’s most famous island, but it is much more than beaches, smoothie bowls, and sunset clubs. It is a deeply spiritual Hindu island in the middle of the world’s largest Muslim-majority country, shaped by volcanoes, rice terraces, ancient temples, surfing culture, jungle valleys, dramatic cliffs, and ceremonies that seem to appear everywhere: on the road, at the beach, in front of shops, and inside family compounds.

History & Culture

Bali’s identity is unique because it preserved a strong form of Hindu culture while most of Indonesia gradually became Muslim. After the fall of the Majapahit Empire in Java, many Hindu priests, artists, nobles, and intellectuals moved to Bali, helping turn the island into a living cultural archive of old Javanese-Hindu traditions.

The Balinese worldview is based on balance: between humans and gods, humans and nature, and humans and each other. This idea is called Tri Hita Karana, and it influences everything from temple rituals to rice irrigation, architecture, family life, and even the way villages are organized.

One of the most fascinating things about Bali is that religion is not hidden inside temples — it spills into daily life. Small offerings called canang sari are placed on the ground, on scooters, in shops, at shrines, and near the sea. They are made with flowers, leaves, rice, incense, and sometimes little snacks, creating a constant feeling that the island is alive with ritual.

Volcanoes, Rice Terraces & Sacred Water

Bali is volcanic, and that explains a lot of its magic. Mount Agung, the island’s highest and most sacred volcano, dominates the skyline and is believed by many Balinese to be the spiritual axis of the island. Mount Batur, another active volcano, is famous for sunrise hikes above a giant caldera lake.

The volcanic soil makes Bali incredibly fertile. This is why the island has such intense green rice terraces, especially around Ubud, Sidemen, Jatiluwih, and Tegallalang. The rice fields are not just pretty landscapes: they are part of an ancient cooperative irrigation system called subak, where water is shared between farmers through temples, canals, and community rules.

Water is sacred in Bali. Temples like Tirta Empul are built around holy springs where people come for purification rituals. The idea is not only to clean the body, but also to reset spiritually.

The Wallace Line: A Biological Frontier

Just east of Bali runs one of the most famous boundaries in the natural world: the Wallace Line. Passing through the narrow Lombok Strait between Bali and Lombok, this invisible line marks a sharp split between the fauna and flora of Asia and Australasia. Even though the two islands are separated by only a few miles of water, Bali's wildlife is distinctly Asian—featuring birds like the Bali starling, and historically, monkeys and tigers—while Lombok marks the sudden beginning of Australasian ecology, where you find marsupials, cockatoos, and entirely different plant life. This is because the strait is incredibly deep, preventing land bridges from forming even during past ice ages.

Beaches & Surf Culture

Bali became legendary among surfers because of its powerful reef breaks and consistent waves. Uluwatu, Padang Padang, Bingin, and Balangan are famous for more experienced surfers, while Kuta, Batu Bolong, Pererenan, Balian, and Seminyak are excellent for varying skill levels. The south of the island feels completely different from the jungle interior: it is drier, sunnier, cliffier, and more dramatic.

  • Best for beginners & cruisers: Kuta, Batu Bolong, Seminyak, Sanur
  • Best for sunsets: Uluwatu, Tanah Lot, Canggu, Pererenan, Jimbaran
  • Best dramatic cliffs: Uluwatu, Melasti, Nyang Nyang, Nusa Penida
  • Best island escape: Nusa Penida, Nusa Lembongan, Nusa Ceningan

The Mystery of the Brilliant Blue Waters

If you've wonderered why the ocean water around Nusa Penida and Nusa Lembongan is so astonishingly, brilliantly blue compared to the mainland, the secret lies in geography and deep-ocean physics. The islands sit right in the path of the Indonesian Throughflow—a massive current that channels water from the Pacific Ocean into the Indian Ocean. Because the channel between Bali and Nusa Penida is incredibly deep, it creates a phenomenon called upwelling. Cold, nutrient-rich, and crystal-clear water from the ocean depths is pushed up to the surface. This deep water lacks the sediment and runoff found near the mainland beaches, resulting in blindingly vibrant, azure-blue waters and unparalleled underwater visibility.

Fun Facts & Curiosities

  • Bali has its own calendar system, and the Balinese year can feel completely different from the international one.
  • The Lombok Strait is so deep that the Wallace Line running through it separates Asian animal species from Australasian ones.
  • The water around Nusa Penida feels noticeably colder than mainland Bali due to deep-sea upwelling currents, which is also why it's so clear and beautifully blue.
  • Nyepi, the Balinese Day of Silence, shuts down almost the entire island for 24 hours — even the airport closes.
  • Many Balinese homes are built like small temple compounds, with separate pavilions for sleeping, cooking, ceremonies, and family life.
  • The island is part of the Coral Triangle, one of the richest marine biodiversity zones on Earth.
  • Mount Agung is considered the most sacred mountain in Bali and is connected spiritually to Besakih, the island’s “Mother Temple”.
  • Balinese dance is not just performance: many dances are connected to temple rituals, mythology, protection, and storytelling.
  • The famous rice terraces exist because of the subak water system, a centuries-old cooperation between farmers and temples.
  • Bali is one of the few places where you can go from volcano sunrise to jungle waterfall to surf sunset in the same day.

Our Impression

Bali felt like several islands in one. Ubud was green, spiritual, artistic, and full of little jungle corners, much like the misty, waterfall-laden mountain ridges of Munduk. Canggu and Pererenan offered thriving café and surf culture but with their own distinct speeds, while Balian gave us a glimpse of quiet, black-sand isolation. On the coasts, Sanur provided calm sunrises, while Uluwatu and Nusa Penida felt wilder and more cinematic, with huge cliffs, shockingly blue water, monkeys, temples, and some of the best sunsets of the trip.

What made Bali special was not just the beauty, but how alive the culture felt. Even in the busy tourist areas, there were still offerings on the pavement, ceremonies in the streets, incense in the air, temple music in the distance, and people dressed beautifully for rituals. It is touristy, yes — but underneath that, Bali still has a very powerful soul.
