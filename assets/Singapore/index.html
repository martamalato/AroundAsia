Singapore

Overview

A tiny country that became incredibly rich

Singapore is one of the most impressive success stories in Asia. It is a tiny island country with almost no natural resources, yet it became one of the richest, safest and most modern countries in the world.

What makes Singapore even more fascinating is that it is a very young nation. It only became fully independent in 1965. Before that, it was connected to the Malay world, became a British trading colony, was occupied by Japan during World War II, briefly joined Malaysia, and then suddenly became an independent country.

Today, Singapore is a city, an island and a country all at once. It is famous for its futuristic skyline, strict rules, clean streets, multicultural society, amazing food courts, powerful economy and one of the world's best airports.

Origins

Who lived there before the British?

Singapore was not empty before the British arrived. Long before modern Singapore existed, the island was known as Temasek, meaning something like “Sea Town”.

It was part of the wider Malay world and connected to regional trading networks. The people living around the island included Malays, fishermen, traders and the Orang Laut, meaning “sea people”, who lived around the waters of Southeast Asia.

Before becoming a British trading port, Singapore was linked to Malay kingdoms and maritime empires such as Srivijaya, Majapahit and later the Johor Sultanate.

History

From Temasek to Global City

Singapore's history is much older than its independence date. The modern country is young, but the island has been part of Southeast Asian trade routes for centuries.

13th Century – 1819
Temasek & The Malay World

Singapore was known as Temasek and was part of regional Malay trading networks. Small communities of Malays and Orang Laut lived around the island, and it was linked to larger maritime powers such as Srivijaya, Majapahit and the Johor Sultanate.

1819 – 1942
British Singapore

Sir Stamford Raffles established Singapore as a British trading post in 1819. Its location between India, China and Southeast Asia made it perfect for trade, and it quickly became one of the busiest ports in the region.

1942 – 1945
Japanese Occupation

During World War II, Singapore was occupied by Japan. This was one of the darkest periods in the island's history and deeply changed how locals viewed British power.

1945 – 1963
Return to British Rule

After Japan's defeat, Singapore returned to British administration. Nationalist movements grew, and Singapore gained internal self-government in 1959.

1963 – 1965
Part of Malaysia

Singapore joined the Federation of Malaysia together with Malaya, Sabah and Sarawak. The idea was to create a stronger regional country, but political and ethnic tensions quickly became serious.

1965 – Present
Independent Singapore

On 9 August 1965, Singapore separated from Malaysia and became independent. It had almost no natural resources, limited land and an uncertain future, but transformed itself into one of the richest countries in the world.

Culture

Is there Singaporean culture?

Yes. Singapore has a real culture, but it is not based on one single ancient ethnic group. It is a modern national identity created from many communities living together.

Singaporeans can have Chinese, Malay, Indian, Eurasian or mixed ancestry, but still strongly identify as Singaporean. This multicultural mix is visible everywhere: in the food, language, religion, festivals, neighbourhoods and daily life.

Chinese Influence

The largest community in Singapore is ethnic Chinese. Chinese influence can be seen in temples, festivals, Mandarin, Hokkien and Cantonese roots, noodles, dumplings and business networks.

Malay Influence

Malay is the national language, and Malay culture is visible in food, music, traditions and neighbourhoods like Kampong Glam.

Indian Influence

Tamil is an official language, and Little India is one of the most colourful areas of Singapore, filled with temples, markets, spices and Indian restaurants.

British Influence

English, law, administration, schools, colonial buildings and many institutions were shaped by the British period.

Language

Why does everyone speak English?

English became important because Singapore was a British colony for many years. But after independence, English became even more useful because the country was so multicultural.

Instead of choosing Chinese, Malay or Tamil as the main working language, Singapore chose English as a neutral common language. This helped different communities communicate with each other and made Singapore attractive for global business.

Four Official Languages

Singapore has four official languages: English, Mandarin Chinese, Malay and Tamil. Malay is the national language, but English is the main language of school, business and government.

Singlish

Locals also speak Singlish, a very Singaporean version of English mixed with Chinese dialects, Malay and Tamil expressions. A classic phrase is “Can lah!”, meaning something like “Yes, it is possible!”

Economy

How does Singapore make money?

Singapore has almost no natural resources, so it became rich by becoming extremely useful to the world. Its biggest economic power comes from services, not agriculture.

Services make up the largest part of Singapore's economy. This includes banking, insurance, shipping, logistics, tourism, aviation, technology, business services and international trade.

Finance & Banking

Singapore is one of Asia's biggest financial centres, especially for banking, investment, insurance and wealth management.

Shipping & Logistics

Its port is one of the busiest in the world because Singapore sits on a key route between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific.

Aviation & Tourism

Changi Airport connects Asia with Europe, Australia and the rest of the world. Marina Bay Sands, Gardens by the Bay and Sentosa attract millions of visitors.

Technology & Headquarters

Many global companies use Singapore as their Southeast Asian headquarters because it is stable, English-speaking and business-friendly.

Trade

What does Singapore export?

Singapore exports much more than people expect for such a tiny country. Some exports are made locally, while others pass through Singapore because it is a giant trading and logistics hub.

Electronics

Semiconductors, integrated circuits and high-tech components are among Singapore's most important goods exports.

Petroleum Products

Singapore has no large oil fields of its own, but it is a major oil refining and trading hub.

Chemicals & Pharmaceuticals

Petrochemicals, specialty chemicals, medicines and biomedical products are major parts of Singapore's advanced manufacturing sector.

Services

Singapore also exports finance, insurance, logistics, consulting, digital services and other high-value professional services.

This is the secret of Singapore: it does not need to be large to be powerful. It became a place where money, goods, ships, planes, people and data all pass through.

Food

The Hawker Centre Nation

Food is one of the best ways to understand Singapore. Hawker centres are open-air food courts where you can eat dishes from Chinese, Malay, Indian and Peranakan traditions in one place.

Chicken Rice

Hainanese chicken rice is one of Singapore's most famous dishes: simple, comforting and taken very seriously by locals.

Laksa

A spicy coconut noodle soup that shows the beautiful mix of Chinese and Malay food traditions.

Chili Crab

Messy, expensive and iconic. One of the most famous dishes to try in Singapore.

Kaya Toast

A classic breakfast of toast with coconut jam, usually eaten with soft-boiled eggs and kopi.

Singapore's hawker culture is so important that it is recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

Fun Facts

Things that surprise visitors

  • Singapore is a city, an island and a country at the same time.
  • It became independent only in 1965, making it younger than many living grandparents.
  • It was once expelled from Malaysia and had to survive alone.
  • More than 80% of residents live in public housing built by the government.
  • It has one of the best airports in the world: Changi Airport.
  • It is one of the safest countries in the world.
  • Chewing gum is famously restricted.
  • It is called the “Garden City” because of its parks, trees and urban greenery.
  • Despite being tiny, it is one of the world's most important finance and shipping hubs.
