Hong Kong

Hong Kong: East Meets West

Hong Kong is one of the world's most fascinating cities. Towering skyscrapers rise between steep green mountains, traditional temples stand beside luxury shopping malls, and influences from both China and Britain can be felt everywhere. Despite being home to more than seven million people, nearly 70% of Hong Kong's territory is made up of mountains, forests, reservoirs, country parks, and undeveloped land.

The Opium Wars & British Rule

Hong Kong's modern history began with the Opium Wars in the 19th century. At the time, Britain was importing huge amounts of tea, silk, and porcelain from China but had little that China wanted in return. To balance trade, British merchants sold opium grown in India into China, creating widespread addiction.

When the Chinese government attempted to stop the trade, Britain responded militarily. The First Opium War (1839–1842) ended with China's defeat and the signing of the Treaty of Nanking, which ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain. After a second conflict, Britain gained more territory, including Kowloon and later the New Territories under a 99-year lease.

For over 150 years, Hong Kong developed under British administration. English became widely spoken, British-style institutions were established, and the city grew into one of the world's most important financial and trading centers.

The SAR: One Country, Two Systems

In 1997, Britain returned Hong Kong to China. Rather than becoming a normal Chinese city immediately, Hong Kong became a Special Administrative Region (SAR).

Under the agreement known as "One Country, Two Systems", Hong Kong would remain part of China while keeping its own legal system, currency, immigration controls, and many civil liberties for 50 years, until 2047.

The big question today is what happens after 2047. Nobody knows exactly how the relationship between Hong Kong and mainland China will evolve. In recent years, Beijing has increased its influence over the city, leading to debates about autonomy, democracy, and Hong Kong's future identity.

Kowloon Walled City: The City of Darkness

One of Hong Kong's most legendary places was the Kowloon Walled City. Originally a Chinese military fort, it became a legal grey zone after World War II. By the 1980s, more than 30,000 people lived inside a maze of tightly packed buildings occupying only a few hectares.

The Walled City became famous for its extremely high population density, unregulated construction, hidden factories, dentists, gambling rooms, and criminal activity. Sunlight rarely reached the lower floors, creating an almost cyberpunk atmosphere decades before the word existed.

Kowloon Walled City inspired countless films, books, video games, and anime. Many scenes in movies such as "Bloodsport", "Ghost in the Shell", and later cyberpunk-inspired productions borrowed ideas from its chaotic appearance. The city was demolished in 1993 and replaced by a public park.

Chungking Mansions

During our stay in Hong Kong, we stayed at Chungking Mansions, one of the city's most famous buildings. Located in Tsim Sha Tsui, it is known for its cheap guesthouses, curry restaurants, money exchange shops, traders, backpackers, and immigrants from all over the world.

In the 1970s and 1980s, Chungking Mansions had a reputation for crime, smuggling, counterfeit goods, and organized gangs. While it could feel intimidating, it was also one of the most multicultural places in Asia.

Today, Chungking Mansions is much safer than its old reputation suggests. It remains famous as a symbol of globalization and inspired the award-winning film Chungking Express by Hong Kong director Wong Kar-wai, one of the city's most iconic movies.

Food We Tried

  • Pineapple Bun (Bo Lo Bao) – despite its name, it contains no pineapple. The name comes from its crunchy, golden crust.
  • Dim Sum – small dishes served with tea, a cornerstone of Cantonese culture.
  • Roast Goose – one of Hong Kong's most famous dishes.
  • Egg Tarts – a legacy of both British and Portuguese influences.
  • Milk Tea – a strong tea mixed with evaporated milk, considered a local classic.

One of Hong Kong's culinary icons is the Michelin-starred roast goose served at Yat Lok. Anthony Bourdain famously described it as one of the best roast goose dishes in the world.

Happy Valley Horse Racing

Horse racing is one of Hong Kong's most beloved traditions. Introduced during British rule, it remains hugely popular today.

Every Wednesday evening, thousands of people gather at Happy Valley Racecourse, surrounded by skyscrapers, to bet on races, drink beer, and enjoy one of the city's most unique social events.

The Hong Kong Jockey Club is one of the largest betting organizations in the world and generates billions of dollars annually. Race nights feel like a mix between a sporting event, a festival, and a giant outdoor party.

Fun Facts & Curiosities

  • Nearly 70% of Hong Kong is mountains, forests, and protected nature.
  • Hong Kong has more skyscrapers than any other city in the world.
  • The city has its own currency: the Hong Kong Dollar (HKD).
  • Most residents speak Cantonese, while English remains widely used.
  • Kowloon Walled City was once the most densely populated place on Earth.
  • Hong Kong appears in countless films including Infernal Affairs, The Dark Knight, and Chungking Express.
  • The Star Ferry has connected Hong Kong Island and Kowloon since 1888.
  • The city contains over 260 islands.

Our Impression

Hong Kong felt unlike anywhere else on our trip. One moment we were hiking through mountains and tropical forests, and the next we were surrounded by neon lights, skyscrapers, markets, and financial towers.

What fascinated us most was the city's layered identity: Chinese yet international, modern yet traditional, capitalist yet shaped by a unique political history. From Chungking Mansions to the horse races, from pineapple buns to Victoria Peak, Hong Kong felt like a place where many worlds collide at once.
